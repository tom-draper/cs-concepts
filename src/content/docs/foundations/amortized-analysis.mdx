Appending to a [dynamic array](/data-structures/dynamic-arrays/) is nearly always instant. Once in a while the array is full, and the append has to copy every element to a bigger block. That one append costs O(n).

So what is the cost of an append? "O(n) in the worst case" is true and badly misleading. It suggests that n appends could cost O(n²), when in fact they cost O(n) in total.

Amortized analysis gives the honest answer. It asks: **over any long sequence of operations, what is the cost per operation?**

## Counting the real cost

Start with an empty dynamic array of capacity 1 that doubles when full. Count one unit of work for each element written or copied.

| Append number | Capacity before | Copies | Cost | Running total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 1, full | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| 3 | 2, full | 2 | 3 | 6 |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | 1 | 7 |
| 5 | 4, full | 4 | 5 | 12 |
| 6 | 8 | 0 | 1 | 13 |
| 7 | 8 | 0 | 1 | 14 |
| 8 | 8 | 0 | 1 | 15 |
| 9 | 8, full | 8 | 9 | 24 |

The individual costs jump around: 1, 2, 3, 1, 5, 1, 1, 1, 9. But look at the running total. After 9 appends it is 24. It never exceeds three times the number of appends.

The expensive appends get more expensive, but they also get **rarer at exactly the same rate**. A resize that copies 8 elements only happens after 4 cheap appends since the last one; a resize that copies 1,024 only after 512.

So the total for n appends is less than 3n, and the **amortized cost per append is O(1)**.

## The savings account view

A helpful way to see why this works is to imagine paying for operations in advance.

Charge every append **3 coins**, whatever it really costs.

- 1 coin pays for writing the new element.
- 2 coins are saved, stored alongside that element.

When the array is full and must double, half of its elements were added since the last resize, and each of them has 2 coins saved. That is exactly enough to pay for copying *every* element, one coin each.

The account never goes negative, so 3 coins per append is always enough. This is the **accounting method**, and the "coins" are purely a way of reasoning. No real program stores them.

## What amortized does and does not promise

    ['Append, amortized', 'O(1)'],

/>

**It is a guarantee about totals.** Any sequence of n appends costs O(n). No probability or luck is involved.

**It is not a guarantee about any single operation.** An individual append can still take O(n). If your program must respond within a fixed time *every* time, such as in audio processing or a game's frame loop, amortized O(1) is not good enough, and you should reserve the capacity up front.

### Not the same as average case

The two are easy to mix up, and they are different.

| | Average case | Amortized |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Averages over | possible inputs | a sequence of operations |
| Depends on | an assumption about the input | nothing |
| Can be beaten by bad luck or an adversary | yes | no |
| Example | [quicksort](/algorithms/quicksort/) is O(n log n) on average | dynamic array append is O(1) amortized |

A [hash table](/data-structures/hash-tables/) insert is "O(1) average, amortized", and both words are doing work. *Average* covers the assumption that the hash function spreads keys evenly. *Amortized* covers the occasional resize.

## Why the growth factor matters

Amortized O(1) depends on growing by a **multiple**. Suppose the array grew by a fixed 10 slots each time instead.

| Strategy | Resizes for n appends | Total copies | Amortized cost |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Double the capacity | about log₂ n | less than 2n | O(1) |
| Add 10 slots | n / 10 | about n² / 20 | O(n) |

With fixed growth, resizes stay just as frequent while getting more expensive, and there is never enough "saved" to pay for them.

## Another example: a queue from two stacks

A [queue](/data-structures/queues/) can be built from two [stacks](/data-structures/stacks/). Push new items onto the `inbox`. To remove one, pop from the `outbox`; if the outbox is empty, first pour the entire inbox into it, which reverses the order.

```python
class Queue:
    def __init__(self):
        self.inbox = []
        self.outbox = []

    def enqueue(self, value):
        self.inbox.append(value)

    def dequeue(self):
        if not self.outbox:
            while self.inbox:                      # occasionally O(n)
                self.outbox.append(self.inbox.pop())
        return self.outbox.pop()
```

A single `dequeue` can be O(n) when it has to pour. But follow one item through its life: it is pushed onto the inbox once, moved to the outbox once, and popped once. Three steps, ever. So n operations cost O(n) in total, and each is O(1) amortized.

That way of arguing, "each item can only be moved a bounded number of times", is the most common form of amortized reasoning in practice.

## Where it shows up

- **[Dynamic arrays](/data-structures/dynamic-arrays/)** and everything built on them: stacks, strings, buffers.
- **[Hash tables](/data-structures/hash-tables/)**, which rehash everything when they grow.
- **[Union-find](/data-structures/union-find/)**, whose path compression makes later operations cheap by doing extra work on earlier ones.
- **Garbage collectors**, where most allocations are instant and an occasional one triggers a collection.
- **Sliding window algorithms**, where an inner loop looks quadratic but each element enters and leaves the window only once.

## Key takeaways

- Amortized cost is the total cost of a sequence of operations divided by the number of operations.
- It lets rare expensive operations be paid for by the many cheap ones around them.
- It is a guarantee on totals, with no probability involved, but says nothing about any single operation.
- It is different from average case, which depends on assumptions about the input.
- Doubling gives amortized O(1) appends; growing by a fixed amount does not.
