How many passwords are there of length 8? How many ways can a delivery van visit 20 addresses? How many five-card hands can be dealt from a deck? Combinatorics answers questions like these by reasoning, not by listing every case.

For a programmer its main use is practical. Before writing code that tries every possibility, count the possibilities. The answer decides whether the approach will take a millisecond or outlast the universe.

## Two basic rules

**The product rule.** If one choice can be made in m ways and a second, independent choice in n ways, the pair can be made in **m × n** ways.

A menu with 3 starters and 4 mains offers 3 × 4 = 12 two-course meals. A 4-digit PIN has 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000 possibilities.

**The sum rule.** If a choice is made from one group *or* another, with no overlap, add: m + n.

Nearly everything else is these two rules applied with care.

## Four ways to choose

Most counting problems are "choose k things from n". Two questions decide the formula.

- Does the **order** matter? (Is A-then-B different from B-then-A?)
- Can an item be **repeated**?

### Order matters, repeats allowed

Each of the k positions can be any of the n items: **nᵏ**.

This counts strings, codes and passwords: 5³ = 125 three-letter codes from five letters.

### Order matters, no repeats

The first position has n options, the second n − 1, and so on for k positions: **n × (n − 1) × … × (n − k + 1)**.

This counts arrangements: gold, silver and bronze among 5 runners is 5 × 4 × 3 = 60.

When k = n, you are arranging everything, and the count is **n!** ("n factorial"): n × (n − 1) × … × 1.

### Order ignored, no repeats

Start from the ordered count and divide by the number of ways to order each selection, k!, since those all count as the same choice. This is written **C(n, k)** or "n choose k".

It counts committees, hands and subsets: C(5, 3) = 60 / 6 = 10 ways to pick a team of three from five.

### Order ignored, repeats allowed

The least common case: choosing 3 scoops of ice cream from 5 flavours, where two scoops can be the same. The count is C(n + k − 1, k).

| | Repeats allowed | No repeats |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Order matters** | nᵏ | n! / (n − k)! |
| **Order ignored** | C(n + k − 1, k) | C(n, k) |

## How fast these grow

Try sliding n and k upward in the demo. The numbers get out of hand quickly, and that is the lesson.

| Count | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 4-digit PINs | 10,000 |
| 8-letter lowercase passwords, 26⁸ | about 209 billion |
| 8-character passwords from letters and digits, 62⁸ | about 218 trillion |
| Five-card hands from 52 cards, C(52, 5) | 2,598,960 |
| Orderings of 20 items, 20! | about 2.4 × 10¹⁸ |
| Orderings of a deck of cards, 52! | about 8 × 10⁶⁷ |

52! is so large that every properly shuffled deck in history has almost certainly been in an order that never occurred before.

**Factorial growth is worse than exponential.** A computer checking a billion orderings per second would need about 77 years to get through all the orderings of 20 items.

## What this means for algorithms

Counting tells you the cost of brute force, the strategy of trying everything.

| The algorithm tries every | Count | Feasible up to roughly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| pair of items | n² / 2 | n in the tens of thousands |
| subset | 2ⁿ | n ≈ 30 |
| ordering | n! | n ≈ 11 |

Every set of n items has **2ⁿ** subsets, because each item is independently in or out. That is why "try every subset" stops being possible at around 30 or 40 items.

The travelling salesman problem, finding the shortest route through n cities, has about (n − 1)! / 2 possible routes. At 20 cities that is around 6 × 10¹⁶. Brute force is hopeless, which is why such problems need cleverer methods such as [dynamic programming](/algorithms/dynamic-programming/) or approximation. See [NP-completeness](/theory/np-completeness/).

Counting also gives *lower* bounds. There are n! possible orderings of n items, and a sorting algorithm must be able to tell them all apart using yes/no comparisons. That takes at least log₂(n!) comparisons, about n log₂ n. So no comparison sort can beat O(n log n), as the [counting sort](/algorithms/counting-sort/#the-limit-it-sidesteps) page explains.

## Combinations and Pascal's triangle

The numbers C(n, k) are called **binomial coefficients**, and they satisfy a simple rule:

```text
C(n, k)  =  C(n − 1, k − 1)  +  C(n − 1, k)
```

The reasoning: pick one particular item. Every selection of k either includes it, leaving k − 1 to choose from the other n − 1, or does not, leaving k to choose from the other n − 1.

Written out row by row, this gives Pascal's triangle, in which each number is the sum of the two above it.

```text
        1
       1 1
      1 2 1
     1 3 3 1
    1 4 6 4 1
```

Row n sums to 2ⁿ, which is the total number of subsets again, grouped by size.

That rule is also a ready-made dynamic programming recurrence, and the safe way to compute C(n, k) in code. Calculating the factorials directly overflows quickly.

## Two principles worth knowing

**Inclusion and exclusion.** To count things that are in A or B, add the two counts and subtract the overlap, which was counted twice: |A ∪ B| = |A| + |B| − |A ∩ B|. How many numbers from 1 to 100 are divisible by 2 or 5? 50 + 20 − 10 = 60.

**The pigeonhole principle.** Put more than n items into n boxes and some box holds at least two. It guarantees that [hash collisions](/data-structures/hash-functions/) exist and appears in many [proofs](/foundations/proof-techniques/).

## Key takeaways

- Multiply the options for independent choices; add the options for alternatives.
- Choosing k from n has four cases, depending on whether order matters and whether repeats are allowed.
- n items have n! orderings and 2ⁿ subsets. Both grow far too fast to enumerate beyond small n.
- Count the search space before writing a brute-force algorithm.
- The n! orderings of a list are why comparison sorting cannot beat O(n log n).
